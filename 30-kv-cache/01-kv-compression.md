# KV Cache Compression

**After reading this chapter, the reader will be able to:**

- Trace the eviction-and-sparsity lineage from StreamingLLM through 2025–2026 learned-eviction methods, naming the specific failure mode each successive paper attacked and the score function each one defines.
- Place the three orthogonal compression families — eviction, quantization, and low-rank/merging — on a single design map, and reason about when each is appropriate, when they compose, and when they break against the kernels and abstractions of a production engine.
- Reconcile the gap between the published-research view of KV compression (rich, near-lossless, plentiful) and the production-engine view (conservative, mostly architectural, dominated by GQA/MLA and FP8 KV) — and articulate where the disagreement is empirical versus methodological.

The KV cache is the single largest piece of per-request state in autoregressive serving, and at long context it dwarfs the model weights themselves [see §00/02-transformer-arithmetic-roofline](../00-foundations/02-transformer-arithmetic-roofline.md). Two structural responses have followed. The *memory-management* response — paged allocation, virtual KV, hierarchical offload — keeps every byte but reorganizes where and how it is stored [see §10/02-paged-kv-memory](../10-engine-core/02-paged-kv-memory.md), [see §30/02-kv-tiered-offload](./02-kv-tiered-offload.md). The *compression* response — the subject of this chapter — argues that not every byte is worth keeping, or that the bytes that are kept can be encoded more tightly than the original FP16 layout. The two responses are partially adversarial in practice: many compression schemes assume a contiguous K/V layout that PagedAttention does not provide, and many low-bit KV formats require kernel-side adapters not every attention backend ships.

This chapter surveys the compression literature along three axes — eviction/sparsity, quantization, and low-rank or cross-layer approximation — and ends with a candid assessment of which have crossed from research into production. The short answer: among the three, only quantization has become a default-on flag in production engines (FP8 KV in vLLM, SGLang, TRT-LLM), and only low-rank in its *architectural* form (MLA at training time) has changed how production caches are sized. Eviction-based compression has overwhelmingly stayed in research, despite a steady stream of papers reporting near-lossless quality at 5–10× cache reduction; the reasons that gap exists are themselves part of the story.

## 1. The eviction lineage

Eviction methods accept a budget — a fraction of the original cache, or a fixed token count — and choose which K/V entries to drop. Every method in the lineage answers two questions: *what score* does an entry need to clear to be kept, and *when* is that score evaluated. The lineage's evolution is largely a sequence of refinements to those two questions.

### 1.1 StreamingLLM and the attention-sink phenomenon

[StreamingLLM](../papers.md#streamingllm) ([Xiao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453)) is the entry point. Its empirical observation: in pretrained decoder-only transformers, a tiny number of *initial* tokens — often just the first four — receive disproportionate attention mass at every subsequent decode step, regardless of their semantic content. Drop them and perplexity diverges; keep them and the model can attend across an arbitrary-length sliding window with no further state. The keep policy is

$$\text{keep}(i) \;=\; \mathbb{1}[\,i \le k\,] \;\lor\; \mathbb{1}[\,i > t - w\,],$$

a union of $k$ initial *sink* tokens and a recent window of $w$ tokens. Cache size collapses from $O(t)$ to $O(k + w)$ — constant in context length. StreamingLLM is most often deployed as an enabling primitive for *infinite-length* generation: a sliding-window-with-sinks layout has no growing cache to compress. The downstream consequence — any token outside the window is forgotten — is acceptable for stream-style workloads (chat, monitoring) but not for QA over a long input.

### 1.2 H2O: heavy-hitter eviction

[H2O](../papers.md#h2o) ([Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14048)) was the first method that *evicts* from a long cache rather than refusing to store it. Its premise: a small fraction of cached positions — the *heavy hitters* — receive most of the attention mass from subsequent queries, and the rest can be dropped with little quality loss. The score is a running cumulative attention sum,

$$s_i \;=\; \sum_{q \in \text{window}} A_{q,i},$$

evaluated greedily at each decode step; at budget $K$, the kept set is the top-$K$ scoring positions plus the most recent ones, and the policy is per-head and online. H2O establishes the score-rank-evict template that the rest of the lineage refines. Reported numbers are roughly 5× cache reduction at under 1% perplexity hit on shorter benchmarks; later critical work shows the picture changes on multi-instruction prompts.

Two intermediate entries belong as a brief note before SnapKV. [Scissorhands](../papers.md#scissorhands) ([Liu et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17118)) formalizes the *persistence-of-importance hypothesis* — pivotal tokens for one query tend to remain pivotal for subsequent ones, so a cheap windowed score generalizes. [FastGen](../papers.md#fastgen) ([Ge et al., ICLR 2024 oral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01801)) is the first head-specific policy: a one-shot prefill profile classifies each head into one of four regimes (special-token, local, heavy-hitter, full-cache) and applies a head-appropriate rule. FastGen prefigures DuoAttention.

### 1.3 SnapKV: prefill observation

[SnapKV](../papers.md#snapkv) ([Li et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14469)) attacks a specific weakness of online heavy-hitter scoring: the score for a prompt token depends on attention queries from *future* generation, which the eviction policy must guess. SnapKV observes that the last $N$ tokens of the prompt — an *observation window* at the prefill/decode boundary — produce attention patterns highly predictive of which prompt positions matter downstream. The score is

$$s_i \;=\; \text{pool}_{1\text{D}}\!\Big(\sum_{q \in \text{obs}} A_{q,i}\Big),$$

a 1-D-pooled sum over the observation window only, computed once at the end of prefill and used to *snap* the cache to the highest-scoring positions before decode begins. This collapses the eviction decision into a single offline pass, removes online ranking from the decode hot path, and produces dramatically better long-context-QA quality than online H2O on the same budget — making SnapKV the dominant non-MLA eviction baseline of 2024–2025.

### 1.4 PyramidKV: per-layer budgets

[PyramidKV](../papers.md#pyramidkv) ([Cai et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02069)) and the contemporaneous PyramidInfer notice that SnapKV applies the same budget to every layer, which is wasteful in opposite directions. Lower transformer layers exhibit broad, uniform attention and need few cached positions to reproduce the same output mass; upper layers attend selectively and need more. The pyramid is a per-layer budget schedule $B_\ell = B_0 - \alpha \ell$, with the largest budget at the bottom — opposite to a naïve intuition that "important" upper layers deserve more state. Reported quality is approximate parity with full-cache LongBench at ~12% retention. [Ada-KV](../papers.md#ada-kv) ([Feng et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11550)) generalizes the idea to *per-head* budgets within each layer, reallocating from concentrated-attention heads to dispersed ones.

### 1.5 Quest: query-aware page-level top-K

The lineage thus far evicts *based on past queries* — every score is a sum over already-seen attention rows. [Quest](../papers.md#quest) ([Tang et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10774)) inverts the question: condition the selection on the *current* query. The mechanism is a per-page summary: every contiguous KV page stores a $\min/\max$ vector across its keys, and the per-page criticality for query $q$ is

$$c_p \;=\; \max\!\big(\langle q, k_p^{\max}\rangle,\; \langle q, k_p^{\min}\rangle\big),$$

an upper bound on the highest attention score the page could produce. At decode time the engine ranks pages by $c_p$ and loads only the top-$K$. Reported numbers are 2.23× attention speedup and 7.03× end-to-end decode latency reduction at long context. Quest shifts the framing from *cache compression* to *cache selection* — the bytes are still there, just not all read on every step — and composes naturally with offload, the design InfiniGen makes explicit.

### 1.6 DuoAttention: trained head specialization

[DuoAttention](../papers.md#duoattention) ([Xiao et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10819)) returns to FastGen's per-head idea but settles it with a training-time procedure. A short calibration run identifies, for each head, whether it behaves like a *retrieval* head (attending broadly across context) or a *streaming* head (attending only to sinks plus recent window). The classification is by output-deviation: ablate the cache to sink+window and measure how much the layer output changes; small deviation marks a streaming head. At serving time, retrieval heads keep a full cache while streaming heads keep only sink+window state. Reported memory reduction is 2.55× on MHA and 1.67× on GQA, with quality matched on the paper's long-context benchmarks. DuoAttention is the closest-to-deployable point in the lineage: offline one-shot calibration, zero runtime cost, static per-head budgets — which removes the kernel-coupling problem of per-step ranking schemes.

### 1.7 Learned eviction: LookaheadKV and ForesightKV

The 2025 wave moves the score from a hand-designed rule to a small trained model. [LookaheadKV](../papers.md#lookaheadkv) (Samsung Research, preprint) trains *lookahead tokens* via a low-rank adapter to predict ground-truth importance scores under a KL divergence loss; the predictor sees the prefill state and outputs a per-position keep probability. [ForesightKV](../papers.md#foresightkv) (preprint) targets reasoning-model traces and trains a lightweight scoring head to predict each position's long-term contribution. Both are SnapKV-style offline-decision methods with a learned score replacing the pooled-attention heuristic. Reported quality at aggressive budgets is competitive with full cache; per-paper evaluations are not independently confirmed at scale across the full long-context benchmark suite as of the time of writing. The unresolved question — addressed below — is whether trained predictors generalize across base models, fine-tunes, and domains, or need redeployment-specific re-training.

The lineage's takeaway: the score function and its evaluation point have improved monotonically — from cumulative-online (H2O) to prefill-snap (SnapKV) to per-layer-pyramidal (PyramidKV) to query-aware (Quest) to head-specialized (DuoAttention) to trained (LookaheadKV/ForesightKV) — and each refinement has moved the quality-vs-budget Pareto frontier outward on the benchmarks the paper authors selected.

## 2. The production-state debate

The eviction lineage is one of the cleanest progressive-refinement stories in the inference literature. It also has not, by 2026, become a default in any major production engine. vLLM does not enable eviction by default; SGLang ships H2O and SnapKV as experimental options behind explicit flags; TRT-LLM offers priority-based offload to host memory rather than eviction proper. Two critical-evaluation papers from 2025 sharpen the picture.

The *Pitfalls* paper ([Pitfalls-KV](../papers.md#pitfalls-kv), [arXiv:2510.00231](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00231), Oct 2025) measures eviction methods on multi-instruction prompts and finds that compression silently drops some instructions while preserving others; the dropped instruction is gone from the output without visible degradation in fluency, and the user sees a coherent answer to a strict subset of what they asked. The paper also documents system-prompt leakage: when budget pressure pushes evictions into the system-prompt region, behavior intended to be invariant under user input becomes input-dependent. Both failures appear at budgets where summary perplexity numbers report parity with full cache. The *Rethinking* paper ([Rethinking-KV](../papers.md#rethinking-kv), MLSys 2025, [arXiv:2503.24000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.24000)) examines whether throughput claims survive composition with PagedAttention and FlashAttention as deployed in real engines, and finds that many eviction methods' kernel assumptions — contiguous K/V tensors, per-step ranking, frequent index updates — do not compose cleanly with paged blocks and the warp-specialized FA-3/FA-4 inner loop, so reported gains often collapse to near-zero in production stacks.

A balanced reading is necessary because the framing "eviction stayed in research" is not universally accepted. Adherents of the learned-eviction direction argue that older heuristic methods (H2O, SnapKV) are the wrong target for these critiques: their failures may be specific to those score functions, not to eviction as a category, and the 2025 trained-predictor wave explicitly targets the multi-instruction and reasoning-trace failure modes earlier work missed. The debate is unresolved as of mid-2026: (a) most production engines do not enable eviction by default; (b) practitioners deploying eviction report workload-dependent quality degradation hard to predict without per-deployment evaluation; (c) adherents argue quality-preserving eviction is achievable and the lack of adoption is partly an engineering-cost problem (kernel rewrites for paged + FA composition) rather than a fundamental quality problem. Every quality-preservation claim in this section is a per-paper evaluation, not an independently confirmed statement; a 2026 capacity-planning default should be a non-evicting cache unless workload-specific evaluation has been performed.

## 3. KV quantization

Quantization compresses by reducing the precision of every kept entry rather than dropping entries. It interacts cleanly with PagedAttention — the block layout is unchanged, only the dtype within a block — and the production-engine integration story is correspondingly simpler. The deep treatment of quantization recipes lives in [§10/04-quantization](../10-engine-core/04-quantization.md); this section surveys KV-specific work briefly.

[KIVI](../papers.md#kivi) ([Liu et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750)) is the canonical sub-FP8 KV method. The empirical observation is that K and V have qualitatively different outlier structure: K has channel-aligned outliers (specific feature dimensions are persistently large across token positions), while V's outliers are token-aligned. KIVI exploits the asymmetry with per-channel quantization for K and per-token quantization for V, both at INT2 with FP16 residuals for the worst outliers. Reported numbers are 2.35–3.47× throughput improvement at 2.6× cache compression with quality within 1 perplexity point of FP16 on the paper's evaluations. KIVI's structural lesson — K and V should not share a quantization recipe — has propagated through the subsequent literature.

[KVQuant](../papers.md#kvquant) ([Hooper et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18079)) refines the recipe by quantizing K pre-RoPE (more amenable to per-channel scaling), using non-uniform codebooks (NUQ) tuned to the non-Gaussian K and V distributions, and keeping a small dense-and-sparse outlier set per vector at higher precision. The paper reports 3-bit KV with under 0.1 perplexity degradation on Llama-2-7B up to 10M-token contexts. KVQuant has not become a production default because the codebook lookup adds non-trivial kernel complexity. [GEAR](../papers.md#gear) ([Kang et al., COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05527)) is the canonical *hybrid*: quantize KV to a low-bit base, capture the residual with a low-rank approximation, and store extreme outliers sparsely — $\text{KV} \approx Q(\text{KV}) + LR + S$ — the most explicit hybrid in the literature.

[TurboQuant](../papers.md#turboquant) ([Zandieh et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19874)) is the 2026 frontier point. The construction applies a random rotation to each K (or V) vector, per-coordinate scalar-quantizes the rotated vector, then keeps a 1-bit Johnson–Lindenstrauss residual for the error. The random rotation makes the per-coordinate distribution near-Gaussian regardless of the original outlier structure, letting the scalar quantizer operate near the information-theoretic optimum. The headline is *near-optimal* KV compression at 3.5 bits/channel for parity quality and 2.5 bits/channel for marginal degradation. The deeper consequence: further improvement on the same KV state without changing the model is approaching the Shannon bound. [See §10/04-quantization](../10-engine-core/04-quantization.md) for placement in the broader quantization map.

A related but architecturally distinct technique is *quantized $QK^\top$ computation* — operating the attention matmul itself in a low-bit format rather than only storing the cache low-bit. SageAttention 1/2/3 ([SageAttn3](../papers.md#sageattn3)) is the leading line of work, progressing through INT8, INT4-per-thread, and FP4-with-microscaling formats; the cache may still be FP8 or BF16, and the savings are in compute and kernel bandwidth on the FA inner loop, not in cache footprint. [See §10/01-attention-kernels](../10-engine-core/01-attention-kernels.md).

Production state: FP8 KV (E4M3 with per-tensor or per-head scales) is shipped in vLLM (`--kv-cache-dtype fp8` with FlashInfer or XFormers), TRT-LLM (default in FP8 builds; `use_fp8_context_fmha` extends FP8 through the entire attention path on Hopper/Blackwell), and SGLang. Sub-FP8 KV (KIVI, KVQuant) remains experimental: the kernel-integration cost has been judged too high relative to the marginal benefit over FP8, and the TurboQuant result raises the bar for what "marginal benefit" means.

## 4. Low-rank and cross-layer approximation

The third axis approximates the K/V tensors with structured low-rank or merged forms; unlike eviction (drop entries) and quantization (reduce precision per entry), these methods change the algebraic shape of the cache.

[Eigen Attention](../papers.md#eigen-attn) ([Saxena et al., EMNLP Findings 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05646)) approximates K and V with their top-$r$ singular vectors, $\tilde{K} = U_K^{(r)} \Sigma_K^{(r)} (V_K^{(r)})^\top$, storing only the rank-$r$ factors. With $r \ll d_h$, the per-token cache footprint scales as $r$ rather than $d_h$, and the attention matmul against the factored form recovers the same compute structure. Reported numbers are up to 40% KV reduction with 60% attention-latency reduction at modest quality loss; quality degrades smoothly with $r$, and the rank-$r$ factors can themselves be FP8, so the method is orthogonal to quantization.

[Loki](../papers.md#loki) ([Singhania et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02542)) uses low-rank structure for top-$K$ key *selection*: project queries and keys into a low-rank space, run cheap top-$K$ there, and load only the selected keys for full-precision attention — structurally a draft pass that proposes which tokens matter, with full-precision attention as verification.

[MiniCache](../papers.md#minicache) ([Liu et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14366)) exploits a well-established observation: KV patterns in *adjacent* layers are highly correlated, because the residual stream changes slowly across depth. MiniCache merges adjacent-layer KV using a magnitude-and-direction decomposition (shared direction, per-layer magnitudes) to halve the depth-axis footprint of deep stacks. Standalone compression is 1.53×; stacked with 4-bit quantization, roughly 5×. Variants in the 2025 wave (CommonKV, LORC, SimLayerKV) extend the depth-axis idea to layer-group sharing.

The architectural counterpoint is Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA), the DeepSeek-V2/V3 design [see §30/03-attention-variants](./03-attention-variants.md). MLA is structurally a low-rank cache, but applied at *training time* rather than post-hoc: the model learns a down-projection to a shared latent of dimension $d_c$ that *is* the cache, with per-token bytes $b \cdot (d_c + d_h^R)$ rather than $2 b H_{\text{kv}} d_h$. For DeepSeek-V3 with $d_c = 512$ and $d_h^R = 64$, the per-token state is roughly 28% of a same-$d$ GQA-8 model. MLA is the production hit of the low-rank category: vLLM, SGLang, and TRT-LLM all ship MLA-aware kernels, and DeepSeek-V3-class models serve at long context with KV footprints competitive with much smaller dense models. *Training-time* low-rank, where the model learns to live with the compressed cache, has succeeded where *post-hoc* low-rank has not — in the same way GQA succeeded where post-hoc head pruning did not.

## 5. Composition and the design map

The three families compose, and the reductions multiply. A DeepSeek-V3 deployment running MLA + FP8 KV achieves roughly $4 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 / (576 \cdot 1) \approx 14\times$ KV-byte reduction relative to a same-$d$ dense MHA model at FP16 — before any post-hoc compression layered on top. Stacking a 5–10× eviction ratio on top of that, if quality permits, would push the effective long-context cache well below the GPU memory required for the *weights*. The structural reason production has so far stopped at MLA + FP8 is a combination of quality risk (eviction silently drops information at workload boundaries that are hard to predict) and engineering cost (kernel-side composition with FA-3/FA-4 is non-trivial), not fundamental impossibility.

The composition stack, from architectural to runtime: (1) the architectural choice — MLA, GQA, MQA, sliding window — sets the per-token-bytes ceiling under which everything else operates; (2) quantization (FP8 default; KIVI/KVQuant/TurboQuant experimental) composes cleanly with paged blocks and is default-on for long-context decode; (3) eviction composes with paged blocks awkwardly and is mostly research as of 2026; (4) low-rank/merging is post-hoc, mostly research, and subsumed by MLA at the architectural level.

What does *not* appear in this chapter: *tiered offload* (HBM → DRAM → SSD → remote) is not compression in the above sense — it keeps every byte at original precision but moves it across storage tiers [see §30/02-kv-tiered-offload](./02-kv-tiered-offload.md); *prefix caching* is also not compression — it eliminates redundant KV computation across requests but stores the result at full precision [see §10/07-prompt-prefix-caching](../10-engine-core/07-prompt-prefix-caching.md); MLA itself, despite being a low-rank cache, is treated as an attention-variant rather than a compression technique because it is a training-time architectural choice [see §30/03-attention-variants](./03-attention-variants.md).

## Current production state

As of mid-2026, the deployed KV-compression footprint is narrow and concrete. The dominant production technique for reducing KV memory is *architectural*, not algorithmic: GQA-8 is the default for new dense models (Llama-3.x, Mistral, Qwen3) and MLA is the default for new MoE models in the DeepSeek-V3 family. Both are training-time choices the inference engine respects rather than runtime compressions it applies; the deepest treatment lives in [§30/03-attention-variants](./03-attention-variants.md). The single layer of compression that the engine itself applies is *quantization*: FP8 KV (E4M3 with per-tensor or per-head scales) is shipped and increasingly default-on across vLLM, SGLang, and TRT-LLM for long-context decode-bound workloads on Hopper and Blackwell, where bandwidth is the binding constraint. INT4 KV (KIVI, KVQuant) and the TurboQuant frontier remain experimental despite strong per-paper quality numbers; kernel-integration cost relative to FP8's marginal-benefit ceiling has gated wider adoption.

Eviction-based compression is, by token volume, near-zero in 2026 production deployments. None of the major engines enables H2O, SnapKV, PyramidKV, Quest, or DuoAttention by default. The underlying primitives — sliding-window-with-sinks for StreamingLLM-style serving, head-specific cache budgets — are present, so an operator with workload-specific evidence of safety can construct an evicted serving path, but the constructions are bespoke. The two 2025 critical evaluations — *Pitfalls of KV Cache Compression* and *Rethinking KV Cache Compression Techniques* — are widely cited in capacity-planning discussions and have set the prevailing posture toward conservatism: assume the cache is intact unless evaluation on the deployment's actual workload shows otherwise. The counter-position, held by adherents of the 2025 learned-eviction line, is that this conservatism reflects older H2O/SnapKV failure modes rather than the achievable frontier; the engines have not staked a default in either direction. Low-rank and cross-layer post-hoc compression (Eigen Attention, Loki, MiniCache, the depth-axis 2025 wave) is similarly research-only — MLA's architectural success has, in effect, consumed the production interest in low-rank caching. The synthesis stack-recipe chapter [see §90/01-production-stack-recipes](../90-synthesis/01-production-stack-recipes.md) takes this picture as given: a frontier-MoE serving stack on GB200/GB300 NVL72 racks running DeepSeek-V3-class models composes MLA + FP8 KV + tiered offload + prefix caching, and does *not* layer eviction or post-hoc low-rank compression on top. Whether the next two years bring an eviction or learned-selection scheme that crosses into default-on production is the open question.
