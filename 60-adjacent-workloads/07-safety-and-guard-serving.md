# Safety and Guard Serving

**After reading this chapter, the reader will be able to:**

- Treat safety classifiers as a first-class serving workload — with their own throughput, latency budget, and false-positive cost model — and place Constitutional Classifiers, Llama Guard 3, AWS Bedrock Guardrails, Azure AI Content Safety, and OpenAI's moderation endpoint onto a single architectural taxonomy of pre-filter, post-filter, and representation-reuse probes.
- Describe watermarking as a *logits processor* (SynthID-Text, SynthID-Image, the Pie programmable-decoder framework), reason about its near-zero compute overhead and its non-trivial key-management surface, and connect the design to EU AI Act Article 50 disclosure requirements.
- Locate prompt injection as an *infrastructure-layer* concern — OWASP LLM01:2025 as the dominant LLM application risk, EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711) as the canonical multimodal indirect-injection failure — and inventory the serving-side defenses (input sanitization, separated prompt channels, schema-enforced outputs, classifier sidecars, RAG privilege separation) that production stacks layer on top of model alignment.

A production LLM endpoint is rarely a single forward pass. Around the model sits a constellation of guard
components: a content classifier in front of the prompt, a topic check against the response, a PII redactor, a
watermark embedded in the output, and — increasingly — an injection detector inspecting both the prompt and any
retrieved context. By 2026 these guards account for a meaningful fraction of total request cost and of end-to-end
latency, with their own SLOs, their own scaling decisions, and their own failure modes. This chapter treats them as
the workload they have become. [See §60/02-structured-output-and-tools](02-structured-output-and-tools.md) for
constrained decoding, which doubles as an output-validation defense, and
[§40/04-trust-boundaries-and-isolation](../40-multi-tenant/04-trust-boundaries-and-isolation.md) for the
GPU-isolation foundations under multi-tenant guard pipelines.

## 1. Safety classifiers as a serving workload

A guard pipeline typically inserts at least two classifier calls per user-visible model call: one on the input
(before prefill) and one on the output (after decode, possibly streaming-incremental). At request rate $\lambda$
into the guarded model, the guard tier sees throughput $2\lambda$ at minimum, higher if multiple classifiers are
stacked — jailbreak detector + harm classifier + PII detector + injection detector is a realistic production stack.

The latency budget is asymmetric. The *input* classifier sits on the critical path of TTFT — every millisecond it
adds delays the first response token. Production budgets target tens of milliseconds for input classification,
which constrains both model size (small encoder-style classifiers are typical) and feature complexity. The *output*
classifier may run incrementally as tokens stream, or as a final post-pass; in streaming mode it can hide behind
decode, in batch mode it adds to time-to-last-token. A 200 ms post-pass on a 600 ms response is a 33% latency tax
on the user-visible total.

The cost model has a third dimension absent from most ML serving discussions: *false-positive cost*. A classifier
that flags 1% of legitimate traffic as unsafe loses 1% of revenue (or 1% of user trust, depending on the
deployment). Production safety stacks therefore optimize an explicit Pareto frontier between false-positive rate on
benign traffic and false-negative rate on adversarial traffic — typically by setting per-category thresholds on
calibrated severity scores rather than binary classify-and-block.

The architectural choices that follow are: (a) what *kind* of classifier (small encoder, distilled LLM, frozen-LLM
probe), (b) where it runs (sidecar on the same host as the model, separate fleet, or fused into the model forward),
and (c) how it composes with structured-output enforcement and watermarking in the same decoding pass.

## 2. Constitutional Classifiers: the classifier-as-API pattern

[Constitutional-Classifiers-2025] (Anthropic; Sharma, Sheshadri et al., arXiv 2501.18837) describes Anthropic's
deployed approach to hardening Claude against universal jailbreaks. The "constitution" is a structured taxonomy of
permitted and prohibited content; classifiers are trained on synthetic adversarial prompts generated by a separate
red-team model to span the jailbreak distribution that production traffic will see. Two classifiers are deployed:
an input classifier that scores the prompt before model invocation, and an output classifier that scores the
response either incrementally or at completion.

The reported numbers in the original release frame the serving cost discussion. Under a baseline configuration, the
classifier overhead added roughly 25% to per-request compute — a non-trivial tax that frames the "cheap" version
(§3) as a serving-optimization problem rather than a research curiosity. The robustness numbers are equally
serving-relevant: against a multi-week red-team competition, no universal jailbreak survived; against an automated
jailbreak benchmark, jailbreak success rate dropped from 86% on the unguarded model to 4.4% with classifiers in
place. The cost-of-safety trade-off is explicit: a 4–5× reduction in jailbreak success rate purchased with ~25%
additional compute.

The serving deployment is a sidecar pattern. The classifier runs as its own service on its own GPU fleet, called
synchronously from the guarded-model API gateway. Both calls are billed against the same request budget. Because
the classifier's input is the same prompt that goes to the main model, its prefill could in principle reuse
retrieved or templated prefixes; in practice the classifier and the guarded model use different tokenizers and
different KV layouts, so cross-model prefix sharing is not exploited.

## 3. Cheap Constitutional Classifiers: representation reuse

The follow-up direction — "Cheap Constitutional Classifiers" — addresses the 25% overhead by recognizing that the
guarded model has already paid the cost of computing rich representations of the input. Rather than running a
*separate* forward pass through a *separate* classifier, the cheap variant attaches a small probe head to
intermediate activations of the guarded model itself. The probe is a lightweight MLP that consumes the
residual-stream representation at a chosen layer and outputs the classification scores.

The reported overhead drops from ~25% to ~4% of per-request compute (hedge: the precise ratios depend on
guarded-model size, the layer chosen for the probe, and which classifier categories are decoded). Two effects
compound: the probe head is orders of magnitude smaller than a full classifier model, and the activations are
computed *anyway* during the guarded-model forward, so the probe rides on already-paid compute.

The architectural implication is that the classifier is no longer a separable service: it runs *inside* the guarded
model's process, reading activations directly. Two engineering consequences follow. First, the model and the probe
are versioned together — a model update may invalidate the probe's calibration, and the deployment must coordinate
them. Second, the probe must execute synchronously with the forward pass on the same device, so the GPU memory
footprint includes the probe weights, and the inference runtime must expose the activation hook (vLLM, SGLang, and
TensorRT-LLM all support custom forward hooks but require integration work).

The deeper lesson is that safety can sometimes be extracted from the representation rather than computed alongside
it. The model "already knows" which inputs are dangerous in a separable region of activation space; the probe just
reads off that knowledge. This is the same insight that drives representation-engineering interventions, applied to
the serving cost ledger.

## 4. Open-weight safety classifiers: Llama Guard 3

[Llama-Guard-3-2024] (Meta) is the open-weight safety classifier that most self-hosted serving stacks integrate. It
is itself a fine-tuned Llama variant — 1B and 8B parameter sizes — trained to classify prompts and responses
against the MLCommons hazard taxonomy (violence, sexual content, non-violent crimes, hate, self-harm, CBRN,
code-interpreter abuse, and others). The classifier emits a categorical safety verdict plus the violated category
labels.

The serving integration is straightforward. vLLM, SGLang, and TGI all run Llama Guard 3 as a regular model behind
their inference loop; the application layer calls it once on the input and once on the output of every
guarded-model invocation. On a single H100 the 8B variant achieves sub-200 ms per classification at batch sizes
typical of guard tiers (a few requests at a time, since the guard tier shares the same arrival distribution as the
guarded model). The 1B variant runs in tens of milliseconds and is the common choice for input classification on
the TTFT-critical path; the 8B variant is reserved for output classification where the latency budget is laxer.

A production deployment must decide whether the guard tier is co-located on the same GPU fleet as the guarded model
(saves networking, complicates KV budgeting) or runs on a separate fleet (simpler scaling, adds a network hop). The
current default is *separate fleet, fewer-but-shared GPUs*: a small pool of H100 or L40S accelerators serves Llama
Guard for many guarded-model fleets, exploiting the lower per-request compute of the 1B classifier. Llama Guard's
open-weight nature also enables on-device deployment for on-prem and edge use cases where sending traffic to a
cloud guard service is not allowed.

## 5. Managed guardrail services: Bedrock Guardrails, Azure Content Safety

The cloud providers ship guard pipelines as managed services, decoupled from the model. The product surface is
roughly the same across vendors: a guard configuration is a named resource that the application attaches to model
invocations.

**AWS Bedrock Guardrails** exposes content filters (six harm categories with LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH thresholds),
denied-topic policies (natural-language topic descriptions the model must not engage with), word filters and custom
blocklists, PII redaction across more than 20 categories (with regex extensions for custom formats), and
*contextual grounding checks* that detect hallucinations against a provided reference. Bedrock advertises
end-to-end guardrail latency in the 50–300 ms range depending on which features are enabled; contextual grounding,
which runs an additional model pass, is the dominant cost when enabled. Guardrails are billed per text unit
independently of the underlying model, which lets the same policy attach to Claude, Llama, Mistral, Titan, or Nova.

**Azure AI Content Safety** is the managed counterpart in Azure. The text and image moderation APIs return severity
scores on a 0–7 scale per category (hate, sexual, violence, self-harm), letting applications set per-category
thresholds. The notable Azure-specific component is **Prompt Shields**: classifiers tuned specifically for
prompt-injection and jailbreak detection, covering both *user prompt* shields (direct injection) and *document*
shields (indirect injection from retrieved content). Prompt Shields is the detection layer used by Azure OpenAI
Service when its built-in content safety is enabled.

The serving lesson is that managed guardrails are converging on a similar feature set: harm classifier, topic
policy, PII redactor, grounding check, injection detector. Differentiation is on PII coverage breadth, on the
integration surface (Bedrock binds to its model API; Azure offers both attach-to-OpenAI and standalone REST), and
on regional availability — which matters for data residency in regulated workloads.

## 6. OpenAI moderation API

The OpenAI moderation endpoint (`/v1/moderations`) is the longest-running production safety classifier API. It
accepts text or multimodal content and returns per-category scores across 11 categories — hate, hate/threatening,
harassment, harassment/threatening, self-harm, self-harm/intent, self-harm/instructions, sexual, sexual/minors,
violence, violence/graphic — plus a top-level boolean flag. The endpoint is offered free of charge for content sent
to OpenAI's models and at near-zero latency overhead (~50 ms typical), which encourages applications to call it as
a pre-filter in front of every user-facing call.

The architectural lesson is that *free, fast moderation* changes integration patterns. When the classifier costs
nothing per call, applications add it liberally, and the false-positive cost dominates the design discussion:
published thresholds and category definitions matter more than inference performance, because applications tune
against the published behaviour rather than against latency. The endpoint is also notable for being *separable*
from the chat-completion API, unlike many provider integrations where moderation is bundled and opaque. Bundled
moderation (Azure OpenAI's built-in filters, Gemini's safety settings) gives less control but cleaner request
paths; unbundled moderation gives more control at the cost of an extra API call to thread.

## 7. Watermarking as a logits processor

Watermarking is the technical mechanism for distinguishing AI-generated content from human-generated content after
the fact. The dominant production approach embeds the watermark *at sampling time*, by perturbing the token-level
probability distribution in a statistically detectable pattern that survives paraphrasing and editing.

[SynthID-Text-2024] (Dathathri et al., Google DeepMind, *Nature* 2024) is the deployed example. The architecture is
exactly a logits processor: at each decoding step, after the model produces logits $\ell_t$ and before sampling, a
pseudorandom function — keyed by a context window of recent tokens and a server-side secret — produces a *score
vector* $g_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}$ that biases the distribution toward a designated subset of the vocabulary.
Sampling proceeds against the perturbed distribution. At detection time, a verifier with access to the same key
recomputes the score vectors over a candidate text and runs a statistical test on whether the observed token
sequence is biased in the expected pattern. If the bias is present beyond the test threshold, the text is flagged
as machine-generated.

The reported quality impact is small — SynthID's published evaluations show that for typical generation settings
the bias is below the threshold that human raters detect as quality degradation. The serving cost is also small:
the score-vector computation is a single hash plus a per-vocab-entry lookup, on the order of $0.1$ ms per token at
$|V| \approx 128\,000$, negligible against decode steps in the milliseconds. SynthID-Text is integrated into
Hugging Face Transformers as a standard logits processor and is deployed in Gemini's text outputs as of 2024.
**SynthID-Image** is the analogous mechanism for image generation: a perturbation in the generator's latent that
survives crops, format conversions, and many editing operations; detection is again a statistical test against a
key, operating on pixels rather than tokens.

The compliance driver is **EU AI Act Article 50**, which requires providers of generative AI systems to ensure that
synthetic content is *marked* in a machine-readable format and disclosed as AI-generated. Watermarking is the
canonical technical mechanism for this requirement. Article 50 obligations apply from August 2026, which has
accelerated production deployment of watermarking across major providers; whether the resulting watermarks are
*detectable enough* to satisfy enforcement (hedge) is an open question that benchmark work is still settling.

The serving operational problem is *key management*. A watermark is only as useful as the key that detects it: keys
must be auditable, rotation must not invalidate older content, and keys must be partitioned across deployment tiers
(per-customer, per-product, per-jurisdiction) without leaking correlation. Production watermarking stacks therefore
include a small key-management service that the logits processor consults at decode time. The key-management is
more operationally complex than the watermarking algorithm itself.

[Pie-SOSP-2025] is a programmable-decoder framework that generalizes the logits-processor pattern: rather than
treating constrained decoding, watermarking, safety scoring, and speculative drafting as separate intervention
points, Pie exposes a single composable decoder API that chains arbitrary processors in a configurable order. The
serving win is that a request needing both schema constraints (§60/02) and watermarking no longer pays for two
separate interception layers; the cost is the design discipline of composing perturbations whose effects do not
silently cancel.

## 8. Prompt injection at the infrastructure layer

[OWASP-LLM-Top10-2025] places **LLM01:2025 — Prompt Injection** at #1 in the OWASP LLM application risk taxonomy,
where it has remained since the list's inception. The taxonomy distinguishes *direct* injection (the user embedding
instructions in the prompt to override system behaviour) from *indirect* injection (instructions embedded in
retrieved content, tool outputs, multimodal inputs, or other model-visible data the user does not directly author).

[EchoLeak-CVE-2025-32711] is the canonical 2025 case study. The vulnerability — patched in mid-2025 — affected a
widely-deployed LLM integration and enabled exfiltration of conversation history through crafted multimodal inputs:
an image or document with embedded text instructions caused the model to leak prior turns into an
attacker-controlled output channel. The failure mode is illustrative: alignment training on the model alone does
not stop indirect injection, because the model has no secure way to distinguish "instructions from the principal"
from "instructions embedded in content the principal showed it." The fix lives at the infrastructure layer.

Production defenses layer at five levels. **Input sanitization and schema validation** normalize untrusted text
(homoglyph detection, invisible-character stripping, language detection) before reaching the model and validate
structured inputs (tool arguments, retrieved chunks) against schemas. This is the cheapest layer and the weakest —
sanitization cannot anticipate every attack — but it removes the bulk of low-effort injection attempts. **Channel
separation between system, user, and retrieved content** is made explicit in modern provider APIs: the system
message, the user message, and *retrieved content* are separate fields in the request, not concatenated text. The
serving stack must preserve this separation through templating (refusing to splice user content into the system
slot) and through the model's chat-template encoding (refusing to render user content as system-turn tokens). When
this separation is honored end-to-end the model can be trained to weight system instructions over inline content;
when it is not, no amount of training helps.

**Output validation via structured-output enforcement** matters when the downstream consumer is a tool, an SQL
executor, or a shell: the *only* safe path is to constrain the output to a schema the consumer expects. An
injection that elicits free-form output is contained; an injection that elicits a schema-valid tool call with
malicious arguments is not. See [§60/02-structured-output-and-tools](02-structured-output-and-tools.md) for the
schema-enforcement mechanic, which doubles as an injection-containment boundary. **Classifier-based injection
detection** — Azure Prompt Shields, the open LLM Guard library, Rebuff — adds models trained specifically on
injection patterns (instruction overrides, role-confusion attacks, encoded payloads); they catch a substantial
fraction of attacks that prompt sanitization misses, and their post-filter mode detects when the *output* contains
injection artifacts (echoed system prompts, leaked credentials). **Privilege separation in RAG and tool tiers**
keeps the retriever read-only and prevents the model from using retrieved content to invoke side-effectful tools
without explicit schema enforcement; tool runtimes are sandboxed (file-system jailing, network egress controls),
and side-effectful tools (database writes, payments) require an explicit confirmation token the model cannot emit
without orchestrator cooperation. See [§60/04-rag-infrastructure](04-rag-infrastructure.md) for the retrieval-tier
side of indirect-injection defense.

The serving observation is that *no single layer is sufficient*. EchoLeak specifically exploited the gap between an
alignment-trained model (which "should" have ignored the embedded instructions) and a multimodal pipeline that did
not separate document-text from prompt-text. The defense is defense-in-depth across all five layers, and the
engineering cost is the non-trivial latency of stacking them.

## Current production state

As of mid-2026, safety classification is a normalized serving workload at all major providers. **Anthropic** runs
Constitutional Classifiers in front of every Claude call, with the cheap-classifier representation-reuse path
deployed to keep per-request overhead near 4%. **OpenAI** runs the moderation endpoint as a pre-filter and
post-filter on ChatGPT and encourages API consumers to do the same. **Google** runs SynthID-Text on Gemini outputs
and embeds safety filters tunable per request. **AWS Bedrock Guardrails** and **Azure AI Content Safety** offer the
same feature surface across many models — content filters, topic denylists, PII redaction, grounding checks, prompt
shields — billed independently of the model inference. **Llama Guard 3** is the de-facto open-weight choice for
self-hosted stacks, deployed on a separate fleet of L40S- or H100-class GPUs running the 1B variant on the input
critical path and the 8B variant on the output post-pass.

Watermarking is shifting from a research preview to a compliance requirement. SynthID-Text is in production on
Gemini; analogous text watermarks have been announced or piloted by other providers, although the specific
algorithms and key-management surfaces remain uneven. EU AI Act Article 50 enforcement from August 2026 is the
production-pull driver that will force consistent deployment, and the **Pie programmable decoders** framework
points toward a future where watermarking, constrained decoding, and safety scoring compose as a single decoder
pipeline rather than a stack of bespoke logits processors. The unsolved part is detection robustness against
adversarial paraphrasing, which the academic literature continues to refine.

Prompt injection remains LLM01 on the OWASP list, and the EchoLeak disclosure is read across the industry as
evidence that the *infrastructure layer* — not the model alignment — bears the load. Production stacks now default
to channel separation in their chat templates, schema enforcement on tool calls, classifier-based injection
detection on multimodal inputs, and privilege separation between RAG retrievers and side-effectful tool runtimes.
The compounding effect of these defenses is real, and so is the latency tax: a fully-defended request to a guarded
LLM with classifiers, watermarking, structured outputs, and grounding checks pays a serving overhead measured in
tens to low hundreds of milliseconds. That is the current price of operating a production LLM endpoint that meets
safety, compliance, and security expectations simultaneously.
